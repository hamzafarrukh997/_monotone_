> **TL;DR:** A standard small-business website takes about 4 to 8 weeks to build. A landing page or a very simple brochure site can be done in 2 to 3 weeks. Anything with custom features, e-commerce, or heavy content usually runs 2 to 4 months. If your site has been "almost done" for longer than that with no clear reason, it isn't a timeline problem anymore. It's stalled, and the cause is nearly always undefined scope, slow feedback, or a developer who has gone quiet. Fix the cause, don't just chase the deadline.

If your website has dragged on for months and you can't get a straight answer about when it launches, you're not imagining it, and you're probably not the problem. Most stalled websites die for reasons that have nothing to do with how hard the work is.

This post gives you two things: a realistic timeline to measure your project against, and a short checklist for restarting one that's stuck.

## How long does it take to build a business website?

Most business websites take 4 to 8 weeks. That covers the full job: planning, design, building the pages, loading your content, testing, and going live.

The range moves with what you're building:

A single landing page or a small brochure site (home, about, services, contact) can be ready in 2 to 3 weeks. A standard business site with 6 to 12 pages, a blog, and a contact or booking form usually sits in the 4 to 8 week window. An online store, a booking system, or anything with custom-built features tends to run 2 to 4 months, and larger web applications can take six months or more (SPDLoad, 2025).

One thing to be clear about: those are working timelines, not clock time. Four weeks of build work can stretch across two calendar months if feedback is slow or content arrives late. More on that below, because it's usually the real reason projects blow past their date.

## What is a website build timeline actually made of?

A website build timeline is the sequence of stages a site moves through from first plan to launch, with each stage depending on the one before it.

Here is what a normal small-business timeline looks like, based on how agencies typically structure the work (SPDLoad, 2025):

Discovery and planning takes roughly one week. This is where you agree on pages, goals, and structure. Design takes about two weeks, turning that plan into how the site looks. Development takes three weeks or more, building the designs into a working site. Review and revisions take about a week of back-and-forth. Then launch, which is quick once everything before it is signed off.

The order matters because the stages stack. You can't build pages that haven't been designed, and you can't design pages when nobody has decided what the site needs to do. A project that skips discovery to "save time" almost always loses that time later in rework. (This is also why timeline and budget move together. ## Why does a website take longer than the quote said?

Most websites run late for reasons that sit on the client's side of the table as often as the developer's. The three big ones are undefined scope, slow feedback, and missing content.

Scope creep is the most common. Across all project types, 52% experienced scope creep, up from 43% five years earlier (PMI Pulse of the Profession, 2018). In website terms, scope creep is the steady drip of "can we also add…" after the work has started. Each small addition sounds free. Together they quietly double the timeline.

Slow feedback is the second. A design waiting three weeks for your approval doesn't pause the project politely. It usually means your slot gets filled by someone else's work, and you go to the back of the queue.

Missing content is the third and the most underrated. Developers can build empty pages fast. They can't finish a site without your text, photos, logo, and product details. A site can sit at "90% done" for a month because nobody has sent the About page copy.

None of these mean your developer is bad. But they do explain why a "3-week website" becomes a 3-month one, and why chasing the deadline rarely fixes it. You have to fix the cause.

## How do I know if my website is stuck or just slow?

A slow project still moves. A stuck project has stopped, even if nobody has said so. Use these signals to tell them apart.

Your project is probably just slow if you're still getting updates, you can see visible progress week to week, and delays have clear reasons you agreed to. That's normal, especially around content and revisions.

Your project is likely stuck if any of these are true: it has been "almost finished" for more than a month, messages take days to get a reply or get none, you can't get a launch date in writing, or you've paid a deposit and gone quiet for weeks with nothing to show. One of these is a warning. Two or more means it's stalled.

The line to remember: past roughly 8 weeks with no working preview link you can click, treat it as stuck and act, rather than waiting politely for it to resolve on its own. It usually won't.

## What should I do if my website project is stuck?

Work through these five steps in order. They move from "recover this project" to "cut your losses," and most stuck sites get unstuck at step two or three.

1. Ask for one thing: a live preview link. Not a screenshot, not a promise. A URL you can open in your browser. This single request tells you almost everything. If a real site exists, you'll have it in minutes. If it doesn't, the excuses will tell you where you actually stand.
2. Ask for a written scope and a launch date. Get, in a message you can save, the exact list of pages and features included, and a specific launch date. If your developer can produce this, the project is recoverable and you now have something to hold them to. If they can't, that's your answer.
3. Find your own missing pieces first. Before you blame anyone, check what they're waiting on. If your content, photos, or logins have been sitting on your side, the stall may be yours to clear, and clearing it may restart everything within days.
4. Set a firm, reasonable deadline in writing. Something like: "Please send the preview link by Friday and a launch date by next week." Keep it calm and specific. A real developer will respond to a clear deadline. Silence after a written one is itself the answer.
5. Get a second opinion before you rebuild. If steps 1 to 4 go nowhere, have someone independent look at what exists. Sometimes 70% of a usable site is already built and just needs finishing, which is far cheaper than starting over. Sometimes there's nothing there, and knowing that saves you from paying twice.

Most stalled projects are recoverable. The ones that aren't are better ended early than dragged out for another three months of the same.

## What a healthy website project feels like

So you know what to expect next time, a well-run build has a few things you can see from the outside. You get a written scope before work starts. You get a rough timeline with stages, not just a launch date. You get a preview link you can check as it comes together. And you get replies within a day or two, not a week. This is the standard we hold ourselves to on every web development project.

Speed on the finished site matters as much as speed getting there. 53% of mobile users abandon a site that takes more than 3 seconds to load (Google, 2017). A site that launches late and loads slow costs you twice. When you judge a build, judge the result, not just the calendar.
