> **TL;DR:** A small business website in Pakistan costs anywhere from PKR 20,000 to PKR 200,000 or more in 2026 — and that range is the whole problem. The number on a quote tells you almost nothing, because "website" describes the outcome, not the work behind it. What matters is what sits inside the price: template or custom design, whether SEO is included, how the content is written, and whether it's built to last a year or ten. This guide breaks down what each price tier really buys.

These price bands are planning examples, not a market survey or a fixed quote. Ask suppliers for current, itemised proposals for your own scope.

## Why does the same website cost 20,000 rupees for one business and 200,000 for another?

Because the word "website" hides more than it reveals. Two sites can look almost identical the day they launch and cost ten times apart, because the price was never about the website you can see. It's about the work you can't.

Ask three people what a website costs in Pakistan and you'll get three answers that don't overlap: a few thousand rupees, a lakh, or more. None of them are lying. They're describing completely different amounts of work and calling all of it the same thing.

That gap is where small business owners lose money — not by paying too much, but by paying a fair price for the wrong thing, and finding out months later. So before we talk about numbers, it's worth understanding what actually moves them.

## Why do website quotes in Pakistan vary so wildly?

Because a quote is priced on the work behind the screen, and that work can differ tenfold for sites that look the same on launch day. The same five-page "business website" can be a free theme with your logo dropped in and AI-generated text riddled with details that don't match your business — or a site designed screen by screen, written for your customers, and built to rank on Google. Here's what actually moves the number:

- **Template vs. custom design.** A ready-made theme is the cheapest and fastest path — you're renting a design thousands of other sites already use. A custom design is drawn for your business before a single line is built, which is what controls how much a visitor trusts you and whether they take action.
- **Number of pages and depth of content.** Five pages is the base. Add service pages, a blog, or location pages and the work climbs — not just in design, but in the writing behind each one.
- **How the content is written.** There's a widening gap between content churned out by AI with no thought and content written by a human with real direction — who your customers are, what they worry about, what makes them buy. The cheapest quotes fill your pages with generic AI text that could belong to any business.
- **Features and integrations.** A WhatsApp button and a Google Map cost little. A payment gateway (JazzCash, EasyPaisa, Stripe), an online booking system, a customer login, or an inventory setup each add real engineering time.
- **Whether SEO is included.** Ask whether the quote covers crawlable pages, useful page titles, clear content, and internal links. A site can be indexed without a separate SEO package, but these basics make it easier for search engines and customers to understand.
- **Speed and mobile performance.** Many customers will visit on phones and slower connections. Building a site that loads well on a modest handset takes deliberate work; skipping it can cost you potential customers.
- **What happens after launch.** Hosting, security updates, backups, and small fixes are either priced in or waiting to surprise you. A low build price often means every future change becomes a new invoice.
- **Who's building it.** A solo freelancer, a small studio, and an established agency carry very different overheads — and very different risks — which is the next thing worth understanding before you read a single quote.

## Should a small business hire a freelancer or an agency?

A freelancer is cheaper and more personal; an agency is costlier but harder to lose — and the right choice depends on how much the website can afford to go wrong. Both are legitimate. They fail in different ways.

A freelancer in Pakistan can build a small business site for as little as PKR 20,000, and for a simple online presence that can be a genuinely good deal. You often deal directly with the person doing the work, decisions are fast, and there's no agency markup. The risks are structural: one person is your designer, developer, writer, and support line at once, so anything they're weak at, your site is weak at. If they get busy, fall ill, or simply move on, your project — and later your support — can vanish overnight. There's rarely a contract, a process, or anyone to escalate to.

An agency costs more because you're paying for a team and the systems around it — a designer who only designs, a developer who only builds, a process that doesn't collapse when one person disappears. That buys continuity: someone is still there in six months when your site needs a change. The trade-off is cost, slower communication through an account manager, and the fact that a bad agency simply hides its weaknesses better than a bad freelancer. A higher price is not proof of a better outcome.

The honest test isn't "freelancer or agency." It's: how much does it cost your business if this website is done badly or disappears? The higher that cost, the more you're paying to remove risk, not to add features.

## What does a small business website actually cost in Pakistan?

A small business website in Pakistan costs between PKR 20,000 and PKR 200,000 or more in 2026 — but reading that as a single range misses the point. It isn't one price with a low and high end; it's three different products that happen to share a name. What separates them is value, not rupees.

### The ~20,000 rupee website: a placeholder, not a tool

At the bottom, around PKR 20,000, you may be buying a basic online presence: a template with your logo and text added. That can be fine if all you need is a link to put on a visiting card so a customer can confirm you exist.

The limits depend on the quote. A template can work well if the content answers real customer questions and the site is easy to use on a phone. The risk is paying for a basic setup while expecting custom design, search-ready content, support, and a site you can grow. Check what is included before judging the price.

### The 50,000–100,000 rupee website: a working sales tool

In the middle band, the website starts doing a job instead of just existing. This is where custom or semi-custom design enters — the site is built around your business, not a stranger's template — usually with proper mobile optimisation, real written content, and at least foundational SEO so you can actually be found.

The value here is compounding: a site in this band is built to convert a visitor into an enquiry and to keep working for you month after month, rather than sitting there as a digital business card. For most small businesses that want the website to bring in customers rather than just prove they're real, this is the floor where the money starts returning something.

### The 200,000-rupee-and-up website: an engine built to last

At the top of the small business range, from around PKR 200,000, you're paying for a site that's engineered, not just designed: fully custom design tuned to how your customers decide, serious SEO, fast performance built in from the start, and real functionality — online bookings, payments, or a system that saves you hours of manual work every week.

This isn't overpaying. It's where a serious small business lands when the website is a core part of how the business runs — taking bookings while you sleep, ranking for the searches your customers actually type, and not needing a rebuild in a year. The value isn't in the number; it's in what the number removes: lost enquiries, wasted hours, and the cost of doing it all again next year.

## Is the cheapest website always a bad deal?

No — but the cheapest website is only a good deal when your expectations are as small as the price. If you need nothing more than an online placeholder this week, an entry-level build is a reasonable start. The trap is buying the cheapest tier while quietly expecting what the expensive tier delivers: a site that ranks, converts, and builds trust. That gap — between what you paid for and what you assumed you'd get — is where the real cost of a cheap website hides.

## What should be included in a small business website quote?

A trustworthy quote spells out exactly what you're getting, when, and what happens after — the vague ones are vague on purpose, because that's where the extra invoices live. Before you agree to anything, a legitimate quote should name:

- Exactly which pages you're getting, and whether the design is a custom build or a template.
- How the content is written — whether the words are written by a human with direction about your business, or generated by AI with no thought and left generic. Ask to see a sample.
- What "SEO" actually means here — basic on-page setup, a full strategy, or nothing at all. Get it in specifics, not as a checkbox.
- How the site is built — the platform, and whether it's a genuine custom build or a page-builder theme, because that decides how it grows later.
- Mobile performance — an explicit, mobile-first, fast-loading build, not an assumption.
- Number of revision rounds — how many rounds of changes are included before changes start costing extra.
- A real timeline — a launch date with milestones, not "a few weeks."
- What happens after launch — hosting, security, backups, updates, and support, and exactly what each costs going forward.
- Ownership — that you own your domain, your hosting account, and your files, in writing. If the developer keeps control of these, you don't own your website; you're renting it from them.
- A written contract — deliverables, payment milestones, and responsibilities agreed before any work starts.

If a developer won't put these in writing, that refusal is the most useful thing the quote has told you.

## So what should you actually pay?

Pay for the job the website has to do — not for the lowest number on the page, and not for the highest. A placeholder and a sales engine are both "small business websites," but only one of them earns its price back. Decide what you need the site to do first — exist, generate enquiries, or run part of your business — and let that choose your tier, because the number was never the real question.

The biggest risk in this market isn't overpaying. It's paying a completely fair price for work that was never scoped honestly, and discovering the gap only after the money is gone. Read the quote, not just the total.
