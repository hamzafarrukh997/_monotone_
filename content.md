#### Article body

> \*\*TL;DR:\*\* A small business website in Pakistan costs anywhere from PKR 20,000 to PKR 200,000 or more in 2026 — and that range is the whole problem. The number on a quote tells you almost nothing, because “website” describes the outcome, not the work behind it. What matters is what sits inside the price: template or custom design, whether SEO is included, how the content is written, and whether it’s built to last a year or ten. This guide breaks down what each price tier really buys.
> 

#### Why does the same website cost 20,000 rupees for one business and 200,000 for another?

Because the word “website” hides more than it reveals. Two sites can look almost identical the day they launch and cost ten times apart, because the price was never about the website you can see. It’s about the work you can’t.

Ask three people what a website costs in Pakistan and you’ll get three answers that don’t overlap: a few thousand rupees, a lakh, or more. None of them are lying. They’re describing completely different amounts of work and calling all of it the same thing.

That gap is where small business owners lose money — not by paying too much, but by paying a fair price for the wrong thing, and finding out months later. So before we talk about numbers, it’s worth understanding what actually moves them.

#### Why do website quotes in Pakistan vary so wildly?

Because a quote is priced on the work behind the screen, and that work can differ tenfold for sites that look the same on launch day. The same five-page “business website” can be a free theme with your logo dropped in and AI-generated text riddled with details that don’t match your business — or a site designed screen by screen, written for your customers, and built to rank on Google. Here’s what actually moves the number:

* **Template vs. custom design.** A ready-made theme is the cheapest and fastest path — you’re renting a design thousands of other sites already use. A custom design is drawn for your business before a single line is built, which is what controls how much a visitor trusts you and whether they take action.
* **Number of pages and depth of content.** Five pages is the base. Add service pages, a blog, or location pages and the work climbs — not just in design, but in the writing behind each one.
* **How the content is written.** There’s a widening gap between content churned out by AI with no thought and content written by a human with real direction — who your customers are, what they worry about, what makes them buy. The cheapest quotes fill your pages with generic AI text that could belong to any business.
* **Features and integrations.** A WhatsApp button and a Google Map cost little. A payment gateway (JazzCash, EasyPaisa, Stripe), an online booking system, a customer login, or an inventory setup each add real engineering time.
* **Whether SEO is included.** Most Pakistani designers leave SEO out of the base price. A site with no SEO does not appear on Google, which means it brings you no visitors and no leads — it’s a brochure nobody can find.
* **Speed and mobile performance.** Over 70% of web traffic in Pakistan comes from phones, often on slow connections. Building a site that loads fast on a cheap Android handset takes deliberate work; skipping it quietly costs you both customers and Google rankings.
* **What happens after launch.** Hosting, security updates, backups, and small fixes are either priced in or waiting to surprise you. A low build price often means every future change becomes a new invoice.
* **Who’s building it.** A solo freelancer, a small studio, and an established agency carry very different overheads — and very different risks — which is the next thing worth understanding before you read a single quote.

#### Should a small business hire a freelancer or an agency?

A freelancer is cheaper and more personal; an agency is costlier but harder to lose — and the right choice depends on how much the website can afford to go wrong. Both are legitimate. They fail in different ways.

A freelancer in Pakistan can build a small business site for as little as PKR 20,000, and for a simple online presence that can be a genuinely good deal. You often deal directly with the person doing the work, decisions are fast, and there’s no agency markup. The risks are structural: one person is your designer, developer, writer, and support line at once, so anything they’re weak at, your site is weak at. If they get busy, fall ill, or simply move on, your project — and later your support — can vanish overnight. There’s rarely a contract, a process, or anyone to escalate to.

An agency costs more because you’re paying for a team and the systems around it — a designer who only designs, a developer who only builds, a process that doesn’t collapse when one person disappears. That buys continuity: someone is still there in six months when your site needs a change. The trade-off is cost, slower communication through an account manager, and the fact that a bad agency simply hides its weaknesses better than a bad freelancer. A higher price is not proof of a better outcome.

The honest test isn’t “freelancer or agency.” It’s: how much does it cost your business if this website is done badly or disappears? The higher that cost, the more you’re paying to remove risk, not to add features.

#### What does a small business website actually cost in Pakistan?

A small business website in Pakistan costs between PKR 20,000 and PKR 200,000 or more in 2026 — but reading that as a single range misses the point. It isn’t one price with a low and high end; it’s three different products that happen to share a name. What separates them is value, not rupees.

#### The \~20,000 rupee website: a placeholder, not a tool

At the bottom, around PKR 20,000, you’re buying a presence, not a performer. This is almost always a free or pirated template with your logo and text dropped in — fast, functional, and fine if all you need is a link to put on a visiting card so a customer can confirm you exist.

The value ends there. It isn’t designed for your business, so it looks like the thousands of other sites using the same theme, and it rarely earns a visitor’s trust. It usually ships with no real SEO, so Google never surfaces it. And increasingly, the cheapest sites are filled with AI slop — generic, machine-written text and stock-flavoured images churned out in minutes with no thought for your actual customers. It reads as filler because it is, and both people and Google are getting sharper at spotting it. A site like this often needs a full rebuild within a year, which means the cheapest option frequently becomes the most expensive one you can buy.

#### The 50,000–100,000 rupee website: a working sales tool

In the middle band, the website starts doing a job instead of just existing. This is where custom or semi-custom design enters — the site is built around your business, not a stranger’s template — usually with proper mobile optimisation, real written content, and at least foundational SEO so you can actually be found.

The value here is compounding: a site in this band is built to convert a visitor into an enquiry and to keep working for you month after month, rather than sitting there as a digital business card. For most small businesses that want the website to bring in customers rather than just prove they’re real, this is the floor where the money starts returning something.

#### The 200,000-rupee-and-up website: an engine built to last

At the top of the small business range, from around PKR 200,000, you’re paying for a site that’s engineered, not just designed: fully custom design tuned to how your customers decide, serious SEO, fast performance built in from the start, and real functionality — online bookings, payments, or a system that saves you hours of manual work every week.

This isn’t overpaying. It’s where a serious small business lands when the website is a core part of how the business runs — taking bookings while you sleep, ranking for the searches your customers actually type, and not needing a rebuild in a year. The value isn’t in the number; it’s in what the number removes: lost enquiries, wasted hours, and the cost of doing it all again next year.

#### Is the cheapest website always a bad deal?

No — but the cheapest website is only a good deal when your expectations are as small as the price. If you need nothing more than an online placeholder this week, an entry-level build is a reasonable start. The trap is buying the cheapest tier while quietly expecting what the expensive tier delivers: a site that ranks, converts, and builds trust. That gap — between what you paid for and what you assumed you’d get — is where the real cost of a cheap website hides.

#### What should be included in a small business website quote?

A trustworthy quote spells out exactly what you’re getting, when, and what happens after — the vague ones are vague on purpose, because that’s where the extra invoices live. Before you agree to anything, a legitimate quote should name:

* Exactly which pages you’re getting, and whether the design is a custom build or a template.
* How the content is written — whether the words are written by a human with direction about your business, or generated by AI with no thought and left generic. Ask to see a sample.
* What “SEO” actually means here — basic on-page setup, a full strategy, or nothing at all. Get it in specifics, not as a checkbox.
* How the site is built — the platform, and whether it’s a genuine custom build or a page-builder theme, because that decides how it grows later.
* Mobile performance — an explicit, mobile-first, fast-loading build, not an assumption.
* Number of revision rounds — how many rounds of changes are included before changes start costing extra.
* A real timeline — a launch date with milestones, not “a few weeks.”
* What happens after launch — hosting, security, backups, updates, and support, and exactly what each costs going forward.
* Ownership — that you own your domain, your hosting account, and your files, in writing. If the developer keeps control of these, you don’t own your website; you’re renting it from them.
* A written contract — deliverables, payment milestones, and responsibilities agreed before any work starts.

If a developer won’t put these in writing, that refusal is the most useful thing the quote has told you.

#### So what should you actually pay?

Pay for the job the website has to do — not for the lowest number on the page, and not for the highest. A placeholder and a sales engine are both “small business websites,” but only one of them earns its price back. Decide what you need the site to do first — exist, generate enquiries, or run part of your business — and let that choose your tier, because the number was never the real question.

Most business websites take 4 to 8 weeks. That covers the full job: planning, design, building the pages, loading your content, testing, and going live.

A single landing page or a small brochure site (home, about, services, contact) can be ready in 2 to 3 weeks. A website build timeline is the sequence of stages a site moves through from first plan to launch, with each stage depending on the one before it.

Here is what a normal small-business timeline looks like, based on how agencies typically structure the work (SPDLoad, 2025):

Discovery and planning takes roughly one week. This is where you agree on pages, goals, and structure. Design takes about two weeks, turning that plan into how the site looks. In website terms, scope creep is the steady drip of “can we also add…” after the work has started. Each small addition sounds free. Together they quietly double the timeline.

Slow feedback is the second. A design waiting three weeks for your approval doesn’t pause the project politely. It usually means your slot gets filled by someone else’s work, and you go to the back of the queue.

Missing content is the third and the most underrated. Developers can build empty pages fast. They can’t finish a site without your text, photos, logo, and product details. A site can sit at “90% done” for a month because nobody has sent the About page copy.

2. Ask for a written scope and a launch date. Get, in a message you can save, the exact list of pages and features included, and a specific launch date. If your developer can produce this, the project is recoverable and you now have something to hold them to. If they can’t, that’s your answer.
3. Find your own missing pieces first. Before you blame anyone, check what they’re waiting on. If your content, photos, or logins have been sitting on your side, the stall may be yours to clear, and clearing it may restart everything within days.
4. Set a firm, reasonable deadline in writing. Something like: “Please send the preview link by Friday and a launch date by next week.” Keep it calm and specific. A real developer will respond to a clear deadline. Silence after a written one is itself the answer.
5. Get a second opinion before you rebuild. If steps 1 to 4 go nowhere, have someone independent look at what exists. Sometimes 70% of a usable site is already built and just needs finishing, which is far cheaper than starting over. Sometimes there’s nothing there, and knowing that saves you from paying twice.

This post explains why that happens and how to find the specific leak on your site.

#### Why does my website get visitors but no enquiries?

Because traffic and enquiries are two different jobs, and most websites only do the first one. Getting found brings people to the page. Converting them into an enquiry is a separate job that the page itself has to do, and that’s the job most sites fail.

Here’s the part that surprises people: even a good website converts a small share of visitors. Across industries, landing pages convert somewhere between about 2.35% and 6.2% of visitors (involve.me, 2025). So if 100 people visit and two enquire, that’s normal, not failure. The problem is when 200 or 500 people visit and nobody does. That gap is almost always the page, not the audience.

#### Is it a traffic problem or a conversion problem?

It’s a conversion problem if people are arriving but not acting, and a traffic problem only if almost nobody arrives at all. These need opposite fixes, so naming it first saves you from wasting money.

A conversion problem looks like this: your visitor count is fine, but the contact form, WhatsApp button, or phone number barely gets used. Spending more on ads here just pours more water into a leaking bucket.

A traffic problem looks like this: hardly anyone reaches the site, so there’s nothing to convert. That’s an SEO and promotion issue, a different post.

Most owners who say “visitors but no enquiries” have a conversion problem. They don’t need more traffic. They need the traffic they already have to do something.

#### What actually stops visitors from enquiring?

Five things cause most lost enquiries: a slow site, a broken mobile experience, no clear next step, no reason to trust you, and an unclear offer. Usually one or two of these is doing most of the damage.

**The site loads too slowly.** 53% of mobile visitors leave a page that takes more than 3 seconds to load (Google, 2017). Half your traffic can be gone before they see a single word.

**It doesn’t work well on a phone.** Most of your visitors are on phones, often on slower connections. If the text is tiny, the buttons are hard to tap, or the form is fiddly, they give up. It isn’t rudeness, it’s friction.

**There’s no obvious next step.** Many sites describe the business and then just stop. No clear “message us on WhatsApp,” no simple form, no reason to act now. A visitor who has to hunt for how to contact you usually won’t.

**Nothing builds trust.** Your buyer has been burned before, or knows someone who has. With no reviews, no real photos, no faces, no proof of past work, a stranger has no reason to believe you’ll deliver, so they don’t risk the message.

**The offer is unclear.** If a visitor can’t tell within a few seconds what you do, who it’s for, and why you, they leave. Confusion doesn’t convert.

#### How do I know which of these is killing my enquiries?

Open your own site on your phone, on mobile data, and act like a first-time customer. Most of the leaks show themselves in the first thirty seconds.

Count how long it takes to load. If you’re waiting and tempted to leave, so are your visitors. Then look at the first screen without scrolling: can a stranger tell what you offer and who it’s for? If not, that’s the leak.

Next, try to contact yourself. How many taps to reach a working WhatsApp or form? If it’s more than one or two, or anything is broken, that’s the leak. Finally, ask whether anything on the page would make a cautious stranger trust you. No proof usually means no enquiry.

If you want a second pair of eyes, have someone who isn’t you do the same test and tell you where they hesitated. The hesitation is the leak.

#### How do I fix a website that gets traffic but no enquiries?

Fix the leaks in order of impact: speed and mobile first, then a single clear call to action, then trust, then clarity. You rarely need a full rebuild.

Start with speed and mobile, because they lose people before anything else can work. Compress heavy images, and make sure the site is genuinely usable on a cheap phone, not just shrunk to fit.

Then give every page one obvious next step. Pick the action you want (a WhatsApp message, a call, a short form) and make it visible without scrolling and repeated down the page. One clear action beats five competing ones.

Then add trust: real photos of your work and team, a few genuine reviews, and plain proof of what you’ve done. Last, sharpen the first screen so a stranger understands your offer in seconds. Small changes here often move enquiries more than any amount of extra traffic.

Most people judge a website by whether it looks nice. That’s the wrong test, because a beautiful site that loads slowly, confuses the visitor, or hides the contact button is a bad website with good paint. Here’s a checklist you can actually use.

#### What makes a website “good”?

A good website is one that turns visitors into enquiries, because it loads fast, is clear, works on mobile, earns trust, and makes it easy to act. That’s the whole definition. Everything else serves those five jobs.

Notice what’s not in that definition: awards, animations, or being the most stylish site in your industry. Those can help, but a site can have all of them and still fail the only test that matters, which is whether a real customer contacts you after visiting.

#### Does a good website just mean good design?

No. Design decides the first impression, but function decides whether the visit becomes a customer. You need both, and most weak websites have the look without the function.

Design matters more than people think at the very start: visitors form a first impression of a website in about 50 milliseconds (Lindgaard et al., 2006). That split second decides whether they stay. But staying is not the same as acting. After that first impression, speed, clarity, trust, and an easy way to contact you are what turn a visitor into an enquiry. A good website wins both moments, not just the first.

#### The plain-English checklist for a good website

A good website passes these eight checks. You don’t need to be technical to test any of them. Go through them on your own site, on your phone.

**It loads fast.** The site should be usable in under three seconds on a normal phone connection. 53% of mobile visitors leave a page that takes more than 3 seconds to load (Google, 2017). Speed is not a luxury, it’s the entry ticket.

**It works on a phone.** Text is readable without pinching, buttons are easy to tap, and nothing is cut off. Most of your visitors are on phones, so a site that only looks right on a laptop is failing most of its audience.

**It says what you do in seconds.** On the first screen, before any scrolling, a stranger should understand what you offer and who it’s for. Clever taglines that hide the point cost you customers.

**It has one obvious next step.** Every page makes it clear what to do next: message on WhatsApp, call, or fill a short form. The action is visible without hunting, and repeated as the page goes on.

**It gives reasons to trust you.** Real photos of your work, genuine reviews, faces of the team, and proof of past jobs. A cautious stranger needs evidence before they’ll risk an enquiry.

**The content is written for customers, not filler.** The words speak to what your customer worries about and wants, in plain language. Generic, machine-filler text that could belong to any business quietly signals that nobody cared.

**It’s findable.** The site has the basics in place so it can show up when people search for what you do. A site nobody can find is a brochure in a drawer.

**It’s secure and yours.** The address starts with https, and you own the domain, the hosting, and the files. If someone else controls those, you’re renting your own website.

#### How do I check if my website is actually good?

Run the checklist on your phone as if you were a first-time customer, and count where you hesitate. Every hesitation is a place a real visitor would leave.

Time the load. Read the first screen and ask if a stranger would understand it. Try to contact yourself and count the taps. Look for anything that would make a cautious person trust you. If you get stuck or annoyed at any step, that’s not a small flaw, it’s the exact point where enquiries are being lost.

A good website is quietly effective, not just attractive. If yours passes these checks, the design is doing real work. If it fails two or three, that’s your fix list, in order.

#### Frequently asked questions

#### What makes a website good?

A good website loads fast, works on phones, makes your offer clear in seconds, gives visitors a reason to trust you, and makes contacting you easy. Its real job is turning visitors into enquiries, not just looking attractive.

#### Does my website need to look expensive to be good?

No. Clean and clear beats expensive-looking. A simple site that loads fast, reads clearly, and makes contact easy will out-perform a stylish site that is slow or confusing.

#### How important is website speed?

Very. Over half of mobile visitors leave a page that takes more than 3 seconds to load, so a slow site loses people before they see your offer. Speed is the first thing to fix.

#### What’s the difference between a good-looking website and a good website?

A good-looking website wins the first impression. A good website wins the first impression and then converts the visitor, because it’s also fast, clear, trustworthy, and easy to contact. Looks alone don’t bring enquiries.

#### Can I tell if my website is good without being technical?

Yes. Open it on your phone as a first-time customer and check load speed, whether the offer is clear on the first screen, how easy it is to contact you, and whether anything builds trust. Where you hesitate is where it’s losing customers.

> \*\*TL;DR:\*\* Before you hire a web designer, ask questions in five areas: proof (can I see live sites you built), scope (exactly what do I get, and what costs extra), ownership (do I own the domain, hosting, and files), process (how long, how many revisions, what happens after launch), and results (is it built for mobile, speed, and search). The answers tell you more than any portfolio. A good designer answers all of them plainly. Hesitation on ownership or scope is your warning sign.
> 

Hiring a web designer feels hard because you’re judging work you don’t fully understand. You don’t need to become technical. You need the right questions, because the answers reveal whether someone is accountable long before any money changes hands.

#### What questions should I ask a web designer before hiring them?

Ask about five things: their proof, the scope, who owns what, the process, and the results. Those five areas cover every way a project usually goes wrong. Work through them in order, and take notes on where the answers get vague.

The point isn’t to catch anyone out. It’s that clear, specific answers are themselves the signal. A designer who has done this well before can answer all of these without discomfort.

#### What should I ask about their past work?

Ask to see live websites they personally built, and to speak to one or two past clients. Screenshots and a slick pitch are easy to fake. A working link and a real reference are not.

Ask: “Can you send me three live sites you built, that I can open right now?” and “Can I talk to a client from the last year?” A confident designer says yes quickly. Vague answers, only screenshots, or “I can’t share client details” for everything are a sign the proof isn’t there.

#### What should I ask about scope and price?

Ask exactly what you get for the price, and what is not included. Most disputes and surprise invoices come from a fuzzy scope, so pin it down before you agree.

Ask: “Exactly which pages and features are in this price?”, “What would cost extra later?”, and “How many rounds of revisions are included before changes cost more?” Get the answers in writing. This matters because added requests after work starts are a leading cause of blown budgets and timelines. Across all project types, 52% experienced scope creep (PMI, 2018). A clear written scope is how you avoid being on the wrong side of that number.

#### What should I ask about ownership?

Ask, in plain words, “Will I own my domain, my hosting account, and all the files, in my name?” This is the most important question on the list, and the one people forget.

If the designer registers your domain and hosting under their own account, they control your website, and a dispute can lock you out of your own business. The right answer is that everything is registered in your name, on accounts you control, and you get all files and logins at the end. Anything less means you’re renting your website, not owning it. Get this one in writing.

#### What should I ask about timeline and process?

Ask how long it will take, what the stages are, and what happens if it runs late. A real process has stages you can see, not just a launch date.

Ask: “What’s the timeline, with milestones?”, “What do you need from me, and by when?”, and “What happens if it slips?” A standard small business website takes about 4 to 8 weeks, so an answer far outside that, in either direction, is worth questioning. Watch for anyone who can’t give you any structure, because a project with no visible stages is a project that quietly stalls.

#### What should I ask about after launch?

Ask what happens once the site is live: who hosts it, who fixes things, who updates it, and what each costs. The build is not the end, and a cheap build price often hides expensive surprises later.

Ask: “After launch, what do hosting, updates, security, and support cost?”, and “If I need a small change in six months, how does that work?” You want clear, ongoing numbers, not a low headline price that turns every future change into a new invoice.

#### What should I ask about results?

Ask whether the site is built for phones, speed, and search, because a site that fails these fails regardless of how it looks. These are not extras. They decide whether the site actually brings you customers.

Ask: “Is it built mobile-first and tested on a real phone?”, “How fast will it load?”, and “Is basic SEO included, and what exactly does that mean here?” Speed matters more than most owners realize: 53% of mobile visitors leave a page that takes more than 3 seconds to load (Google, 2017). If the answer to any of these is a shrug, the site will look fine and still lose you customers.

#### Which answers are red flags?

The warning signs are refusal to share live work, no written scope, keeping your domain or hosting, and guaranteed Google rankings. Any one of these should make you slow down before signing.

A good designer welcomes these questions, because answering them well is how they win serious clients. Someone who gets defensive, stays vague on ownership, or promises guaranteed page-one results has answered the most important question of all: whether to hire them.

#### Frequently asked questions

#### What should I ask a web designer before hiring them?

Ask about five things: proof (live sites they built), scope (exactly what’s included and what costs extra), ownership (do you own the domain, hosting, and files), process (timeline, revisions, what happens if it’s late), and results (mobile, speed, and SEO). Clear answers are the signal.

#### What is the most important question to ask a web designer?

Whether you will own your domain, hosting, and files in your own name. If the designer keeps these under their account, they control your website and can lock you out. Get ownership confirmed in writing.

#### How many revisions should be included?

There’s no single right number, but it should be stated clearly before you start. Ask how many rounds of changes are included and what happens after that, so revisions don’t turn into surprise charges.

#### Should I get a contract with a web designer?

Yes. A written contract with scope, price, timeline, revisions, and ownership protects both sides. If a designer won’t put the deal in writing, that refusal is itself a reason not to hire them.

#### How can I tell a good web designer from a bad one before paying?

By how they answer these questions. A good one gives specific, plain answers and welcomes a contract. A bad one is vague on scope and ownership, shares no live work, or guarantees Google rankings.
